## What life is like

People admire work that welcomes another person's touch and patient teachers who can share a task without taking it over. Children acquire enduring adult and peer relationships through making things together. Elders can teach, create, socialize, or simply visit; teaching is not a condition of belonging. Households differ over portable keepsakes, living communal works, ambitious exhibitions, and the value of keeping familiar surroundings. The chosen tone is warm domestic observation with restrained humor.

## Who benefits / who is harmed / what it costs

Children gain accessible creative space and relationships beyond their household. Adults and elders gain a shared place to meet across schedules. Different bodies require adjustable working surfaces and consideration rather than identical technique. Residents pay for a large habitable room and ongoing cleaning and water recovery. Persistent preservation can crowd out new makers; replacement can wound people who still use an old work as a place of companionship. Neither choice is secretly fraudulent. Expansion purchases room with additional labor, which enthusiasts may promise more readily than they perform.

## Hooks left

An exhibition would separate teaching partners from their shared original; firing a deceased maker's landscape would end a younger contributor's ongoing work; an available freight hall offers expansion at the cost of maintenance shifts. These remain unresolved personal and neighborhood decisions.
