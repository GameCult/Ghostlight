# The Silt-Crown Receivership

The first thing the new managers did at Seven Weirs mine was stop the pumps in the lower galleries. The tunnels flooded. Workers expected the next order to close the mine, but their wages kept arriving. Crews were sent upstream to repair channels the old management had allowed to fill with waste.

The new managers answered to a group of goblin traders. The traders had bought enough of Seven Weirs' debt to take control when it missed two deliveries of mana crystal. Their demands were expensive and oddly specific: flood this gallery, leave that seam untouched, clear one spring before reopening another bore.

## Buying the Mine

Seven Weirs had borrowed money to build its rail and drainage works. Its lenders received bronze bonds promising payment from future crystal deliveries. The contract allowed a majority of bondholders to appoint a temporary manager after repeated failure to pay.

The goblin traders bought the bonds cheaply from lenders worried about the mine. When two bores became impassable and deliveries failed, they presented the bonds and invoked the contract. The court accepted the traders as creditors. It had no reason to decide whether a dungeon could own anything.

Seven Weirs' former owners say the traders arranged the blockages. They also accuse them of buying the bonds with treasure stolen from the company's workings. The traders maintain that their expeditions stayed outside the lease. Both sides need the old tunnel surveys, some of which no longer match the passages underground.

## Who Paid the Traders?

The traders' expedition books describe repeated journeys into Silt-Crown, a flooded dungeon beneath the surrounding watershed. They found easy routes to valuable crystals. Later visits revealed arrangements of stones resembling marks on the bond certificates. They returned with copies of their purchases; the next expedition found a different route open.

Several of the traders believe something below is guiding them. One [[Dwarven Industry#Rune Colleges|Rune College]] researcher who has seen their books thinks the exchanges might support the fringe argument that old [[Dungeon Cores|cores]] can think. The books have not been independently checked. They could record a discovery, a misunderstanding, or an elaborate cover for the traders' own scheme.

The mineworkers hear arguments about drainage and creditors. They have not been told that their employers may be taking instructions from the mine itself.

## Keeping It Open

The traders cannot simply stop extraction. Under the contract, their manager must keep the mine operating and pay its remaining debts. Lower output already means less crystal for nearby towns and missed interest for the pension funds that still hold some bonds.

Money from the dungeon expeditions covers part of the shortfall. The traders have also approved cutting farther from the flooded galleries. They do not know whether those new workings belong to another part of the same dungeon, or whether that question means anything at all.

Seven Weirs can recover control by paying what it owes. Its former owners are seeking new finance. The traders need enough income to keep their position, while the instructions they believe they receive keep reducing the amount they can safely mine.

## Open Questions

- What happens when the next arrangement underground appears to demand a closure the traders cannot afford?
- Can an independent expedition repeat the exchanges, or do they work only for the traders who profit from them?
- If Seven Weirs repays its debt, will the flooded galleries become passable for the old management?

# Workskin

In some dwarven workshops, an apprentice polishing a finished pump is taught to leave one copper seam alone. Blue-green lichen grows there. Scrubbing it off earns the same rebuke as throwing away a useful tool.

The makers call it **workskin**. They keep patches on old copper scraps and give shavings to apprentices leaving to start their own shops. A repairer may still be tending a strain their grandmother used. It grows differently in a damp Hold than beside a dry furnace, and the advice passed down with it includes where to put it, how to feed it, and when to trim it back.

The lichen feeds on small leaks of mana. [[Mana and Pattern|The work an engine does leaves traces in that flow]], which affect how the growth colours and thickens. An artisan who knows the machine and its skin can sometimes notice strain before a part fails. Much of that knowledge is taught beside a running engine: this ridge appeared when the pump began labouring; that pale patch means the skin itself needs care.

The practice carries mistakes and superstition along with useful experience. Some makers read more into a discoloured patch than it can tell them. A shaving brought from another Hold may behave quite differently.

## The New Finish

The [[Dwarven Industry#Forge Consortiums|Forge Consortiums]] sell engines with smooth, sealed housings and bright surfaces that wipe clean. They waste less mana and travel well. In a showroom beside one of them, an artisan's carefully tended pump looks old and dirty.

The new casing has no exposed seam for workskin. Its cleaning instructions treat any growth as something to remove. Designers give little thought to what a local repairer learned from a particular patch over thirty years. A useful habit disappears with the recess that used to hold it.

Some artisans scratch a strip through the finish and try a shaving there. Others keep their old engines until parts become too hard to make. Their apprentices learn the new models, and the copper scraps on the workshop shelf gradually dry out.

## Open Questions

- An old repairer recognizes a troubling colour in the last skinned pump on a street. How can they explain the warning to people whose new machines show nothing?
- A workshop loses its starter patch. Will a rival share a strain the two families once inherited from the same teacher?
- What happens when an apprentice's scratched seam helps the workskin grow but damages the new engine's casing?

# The Second Answer

Veyra Underbeam was a dwarven bridge-builder who became a minor [[Gods and Religion|goddess]] after her death. Builders ask her to help them make safe crossings. Travellers pray that they and their families will reach the other side.

Her blessings have made her temples attractive to industrial patrons. A company building a railway can pay for priests, maintain a shrine, and promise regular offerings from its work crews. In return, it wants blessings for its bridges. That money keeps temples open and supports work people depend on.

Veyra needs worship, and its form shapes her. She understands what the bargain may do to her: if her worship centres increasingly on approving new bridges, that devotion will shape the goddess she becomes. She wants to help the builders. She also wants people to keep coming to her with questions about whether a crossing should open, who it serves, and who might be endangered by it.

## What a Blessing Teaches

A blessing can change more than the bridge. An owner may present it as Veyra's approval of the whole venture, including decisions she was never asked to judge. Workers may hesitate to question a project their goddess appears to favour.

Veyra considers that response when deciding whether to act. A miracle within her power can still encourage a form of devotion she does not want. Refusing it can delay useful work and disappoint people whose prayers were sincere.

Her high priests and paladins understand the difficulty. They choose which prayers to teach, which deeds to celebrate, and what a temple says a miracle means. They speak with Veyra about those choices. Their disagreements concern how to serve their communities and what kind of goddess their worship helps her remain.

## The Second Answer

Some of Veyra's temples have begun requiring a second hearing before accepting a patron's bridge commission. The builders first bring their plans and ask for help. Then people who will use the crossing, maintain it, or live beneath it bring their concerns. The priests call this the **Second Answer**.

A railway owner may arrive expecting a blessing for sound stone and good workmanship. The second hearing may bring a ferryman whose livelihood will vanish, a village that cannot afford the planned toll, or workers afraid the opening date leaves too little time for inspection.

Veyra's domain does not give her the power to solve every grievance. Her clergy can nevertheless refuse to present a blessing for the bridge as divine approval of everything built around it. They may ask the patron to change its plans before the temple accepts the work. The hearing also teaches worshippers that approaching Veyra includes listening to people affected by a crossing.

## Paying for the Temple

Patrons sometimes take their offerings elsewhere. A priest who refuses a commission may have to dismiss assistants or close a roadside shrine. Families who depended on that shrine do not necessarily welcome the principle that cost them its care.

Other priests argue that steady patronage lets them do more good, and that careful teaching can prevent a useful blessing from becoming an endorsement. Paladins carrying the concerns of distant communities may be less willing to trust the distinction than clergy who know the company's builders personally.

Veyra shares the consequences. She can insist on a form of worship and watch people leave it. She can accept a narrower role to keep helping them. Her closest servants must decide what to ask of her as well as what to ask of themselves.

## Open Questions

- Will a temple keep the second hearing when its largest patron threatens to fund another shrine?
- What happens when Veyra wants to bless a crossing her high priest believes will teach the wrong lesson about her?
- Can a paladin persuade a community to change a beloved rite because of what it is encouraging their goddess to become?
