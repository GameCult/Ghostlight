description: A candidate lakeshore culture whose households spend the dry season apart and the wet season together.

*Canon candidate; proposed locality and culture.*

Around Lethmere, a shallow freshwater lake in the surface lowlands, families carry their doors uphill when the wet season approaches. The stone cottage shells withstand seasonal flooding; good wooden doors are too costly to leave soaking. The doors fit both their lakeside cottages and their rooms in the large stone houses above the flood line. Children recognize home by the scratches beside the latch, even when home now contains twenty more people and somebody has taken their usual breakfast seat.

The shorefolk are mostly [[Halflings]], with long-established [[Humans|human]] families and households of other ancestry. They share a seasonal way of living, local recipes, and attachments to particular stretches of shore. Newcomers usually learn these through a partner, a rented garden, or work in an upper house. Nobody expects every resident to have lake-born grandparents.

Lethmere spreads across broad clay shelves during the rains and retreats in the dry months, exposing damp ground where beans, onions, and quick greens can grow. Families return to small cottages on low gravel ridges, then cultivate the newly uncovered plots below them. Reed beds supply roofing and basket makers; fishing continues from the deeper channels. Produce, smoked fish, and reed mats travel by cart to inland markets. The upper houses keep kilns on their dry slopes, turning lakeshore clay into cooking pots during the wet months. Grain, iron tools, and much of the firewood come back along the same road.

## Two seasons of company

An upper house belongs to several households, each with its own rooms. Its broad kitchen and drying loft are shared. Over generations, families have built additions for grown children or sold rooms to unrelated neighbours. Some houses contain close kin; others have become a collection of people whose grandparents bought sensibly and disliked one another.

Through the dry season, visiting means walking along the shore with something to eat. People relish the space to quarrel privately, keep odd hours, and try recipes without an audience. In the rains, the same families share ovens, mend fishing gear, shape clay, and teach children in the warm kitchen. Those who remain uphill all year tend the kilns and gardens above the water. They are often the people children run to first on returning.

Shorefolk admire a host who notices when a guest wants company and when they want a chair by themselves. Children learn to pack a room so another person can carry it: handles outward, heavy things divided, sharp tools wrapped. Moving day ends with a meal made from whatever each household refused to pack. The combination is rarely repeated deliberately.

Wet-season pottery gives the long evenings an ambition beyond waiting for spring. Each upper house has favourite shapes and glazes. Young potters bring experiments to the first dry-season market, where a handsome pouring lip can win more attention than an expensive colour. A few want to sell far beyond the lake. Others treasure the winter when they finally made six bowls that stacked.

Before the doors go downhill again, households bring a little of their stored food to the upper house's shrine. Their [[Gods and Religion|worship]] thanks the gods they ordinarily petition for shelter, craft, and safe travel. Prayers include the names of people who will spend the season elsewhere. Families disagree about how long an absent name should remain in the prayer; a daughter opening a distant shop may hear affection where her brother hears a summons home.

## Staying put

Several prosperous gardeners want to build permanent homes on raised ground beside the lower gardens. Grandparents with painful knees support them. So do young couples tired of conducting a marriage within hearing of the winter kitchen. They propose paying for the earthwork together and keeping their rooms uphill for visits.

Their neighbours worry about losing the people who fire the communal oven, teach pottery, and make wet months sociable. Keeping an unused room also leaves less space for someone who needs it. A lakeside home would spare its owners two moves each year, but a household selling its upper rooms might later struggle to return. Some families are trying shorter winter stays before deciding what to build.

## Unresolved hooks

- A potter receives a year-round offer from an inland workshop just as her upper house asks her to teach its children through the rains.
- Two sisters agree to fund a raised cottage, then discover that only one wants to sell their rooms uphill.
- A newcomer proposes turning an empty drying loft into private rooms; the winter musicians have practised there for thirty years.
