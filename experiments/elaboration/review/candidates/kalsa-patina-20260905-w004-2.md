# The Bowl Asks After You

Udrem divides the cooked food before leaving home. One portion goes to his daughter's table; the other goes to Chavi, whose serving bowl has been asking whether he has found better company. He chooses pieces of similar size for each. It takes longer than he would care to explain.

At Reed Elbow, a small cluster of [[Spoilers/Polities/Shamanism - Papsenai|papsenai farming households]] beside an irrigation bend in the southern gulf, friends give familiar possessions opinions about one another. People invent the voices. A basket may admire somebody's strength extravagantly after being carried home full; a mat may remember the guest who stayed talking until everyone else had gone to sleep. The next encounter supplies another line.

Neighbours tend the channel, dry crops and share a shelter for sorting their harvests. Their belongings pass through enough hands to acquire histories worth teasing about. Someone can answer a complaint while working, then spend the rest of the sorting imagining a better answer. Some favour elaborate quarrels. Others prefer a cup that quietly approves of the visitor who always turns its chipped edge away from their mouth.

Chavi's wide bowl became jealous when Udrem praised another household's cooking over a meal. She pretended to console it. He took a generous helping and said reconciliation required privacy. Since then they have accused it of listening from the shelf, forgetting compliments and remembering portions. Chavi likes that Udrem can make her laugh with a slight turn of an old phrase. Udrem likes that she remembers which phrase.

A different guest can eat from the same bowl without meeting that character. Chavi's youngest nephew, however, has heard its grievances since he was small. He learned the earliest stories by interrupting meals to ask what everyone was laughing at. Now he can answer before Udrem does, which pleases Chavi more consistently than it pleases Udrem.

Lately Udrem has been eating often with his daughter, who has established her own household nearby. They are trying different ways of cooking the food they grow, and he wants to be there when she tastes the result. He still enjoys Chavi's table. But the bowl's complaints have become frequent enough that he brings food even when he cannot stay. Chavi takes the portions as a new extravagance in their old exchange. Udrem has started selecting them as compensation.

Chavi really does miss him. A delivered dish is pleasant, but it leaves nobody across the table to answer her. She also remembers long stretches when he visited without being asked. Having to request each meal feels like losing something, even if he comes. Udrem would rather hear when she wants him than keep guessing whether a clever reply has settled anything. He has not yet told her that dividing the food is becoming less enjoyable than cooking it.

The nephew wants the bowl as his inheritance when Chavi eventually passes her possessions on. He says so while she is using it, and she is delighted that he wants this ordinary thing so much. He imagines serving friends from it and answering its complaints for himself. There is no illness or imminent death behind the request. For now he asks to borrow it for a meal he will cook for Chavi and Udrem.

Udrem has never eaten at his table. He wants to try the food, but hearing the nephew perform the familiar jealousy makes him reluctant to answer in kind. He would like another friendship with its own jokes. Chavi wants to hear the two of them make the old bowl funny together. The nephew has prepared a reply he is proud of; he also has a dish he wants them to taste before they start talking about meals he was too young to remember.
