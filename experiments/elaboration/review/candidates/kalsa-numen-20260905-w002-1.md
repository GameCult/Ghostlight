# Prayers in Another Mouth

In the cloth-mending quarter of Reed Bend, a small market settlement in Kalsa's habitable twilight belt, some households exchange prayers before their common rest. A wife gives her husband the words she wants spoken to God. He says them in the first person while she listens. Then they change places. People who spend most of their lives telling one another what they ought to want sometimes hear a startlingly unfamiliar voice coming out of someone they love.

These families belong to one local monotheistic tradition among the many [[Spoilers/Polities/Null and Control Descendants|Control-descended communities]]. They address the **One Listener**, whom they believe hears every person without requiring a priest to make that person important. Their worship includes neighbours and spouses of other ancestry, with or without an expressed Gift. They know their grandparents' prayers and stories of earlier settlements; they possess no agreed account of their people's ancient creation.

Reed Bend's menders repair clothing and travelling covers for the surrounding farming settlements and passing traders. Several related families rent adjoining workrooms. Their devotion travels easily between rented homes: it needs a willing second voice, and the gathering can fit into the pause after work. Its timing follows household rest, not a sunset that arrives each day; [[Spoilers/Foundations/World|Kalsa's sun]] does not cross their sky that way.

## Hearing the wish

The prayer begins with an inherited invocation, followed by words chosen in ordinary speech. The distinction between formal liturgy and changing [[Spoilers/Foundations/Language and Reckoning|everyday language]] matters here. Children first learn to carry small, exact requests: let my tooth stop hurting; let me win when we race again. Adults admire someone who can speak an embarrassing hope without turning solemnity into pity. An excellent speaking voice helps, but a fine reciter who improves everyone's requests soon runs short of partners.

The listener may correct a missed word. The speaker may refuse a prayer before beginning it. Neither can quietly replace the other's wish with a lesson. Some couples exchange only familiar blessings; friends sometimes entrust each other with hopes they cannot yet say at home. A person who wants privacy can pray alone. Relatives still notice when a once-eager partner repeatedly chooses solitude.

At larger gatherings, pairs speak softly at the same time. Individual sentences become hard to follow. Worshippers call this the Listener's nearness: each person can hear how much remains beyond their own attention, and imagine that nothing is lost to God. Some close their eyes; others watch the mouth carrying their words. No answer or magical effect is promised. The crowded murmur is enough to bring people back.

## A name in the thanksgiving

Mera mends harness covers beside her husband, Tal. After a fever, she credits her recovery to [[Spoilers/Institutions/God-Cult Covenants|treatment at a neighbouring god's shrine]]. She wants Tal to speak her thanksgiving, including that god's name. She has already paid the shrine's charges and thanked its attendants. Leaving its patron unnamed now feels furtive.

Tal willingly carried her there. He believes powerful gods can heal, yet reserves worship for the One Listener. He offers to thank the Listener for the shrine's help. Mera says those are his words. He hears her request as asking him to worship someone else; she hears his correction as claiming that her recovery belongs to his faith before it belongs to her.

Their aunt uses other gods' names freely when recounting kindness. A neighbouring family will thank mortal healers but refuses divine names within prayer. The local reciter says a borrowed prayer remains the petitioner's, a position Tal suspects is easier to maintain when the troublesome name belongs to somebody else's marriage.

Mera and Tal still exchange the short blessing over sleep. Her longer thanksgiving lies folded beneath her sewing weights.
