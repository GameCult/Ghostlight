# The Guest Harvest

In a proposed cluster of papsenai farming households called the Bent Channel, an older grower can ask to become a guest at one harvest. Their younger kin raise a small patch without their labor or advice, cook from it, and invite them to eat. People begin planning these meals while the prospective guest is still vigorous enough to interfere. Waiting until someone cannot leave their bed is generally considered to have missed the pleasure of the thing.

The Bent Channel lies among the southern gulf farms of the [[Spoilers/Polities/Shamanism - Papsenai|papsenai]], beside an ordinary irrigation bend. Its households grow grain and keep draft animals; they share channel maintenance and sell surplus through neighboring stores. The guest patch uses existing household ground. Its crop supplies a meal, leaving the household's main food and seed reserves outside the experiment.

Children learn what is happening when an aunt sends them back with a question unanswered. They must ask the people tending the patch. The guest can offer help if the crop is failing, but accepting it means trying again at another planting. Neighbors sometimes rescue the meal with their own knowledge. Being able to find help is admired almost as much as knowing the answer oneself.

Guests have favorite ways of spending this interval. Some visit relatives whose doors they usually reach carrying a repair job. Others make small clay animals, teach songs, or continue working on another field. At the meal, the younger growers describe a decision the guest would have made differently. The guest gets to taste the result before commenting. Families remember especially good food, especially bad advice withheld with difficulty, and the first meal at which somebody's mother asked for another serving.

For several households, this is preparation for death. Kalsan [[Spoilers/Institutions/Death and Binding Law|mortuary traditions]] include both appeals for ancestral protection and invitations to relinquish the living world. At the Bent Channel, followers of a local release teaching believe the dead can become trapped in their own conviction that nobody else will manage. Feeding an elder as a guest gives that person something to remember when descendants call. Their teachers describe release as a difficult sacred trust in the living, and hope to practice it before fear and grief arrive.

Other participants honor ancestors who remain near their families. They hold guest harvests too: a parent who has learned to accept care may become a kinder guardian than one who expects perpetual obedience. These families invite elders to choose work they would welcome after death. Neither teaching can establish what a particular dead person will become. Household observance also does not settle obligations to a god under existing [[Spoilers/Institutions/God-Cult Covenants|cult covenants]].

Davru has asked for her guest harvest after decades tending the household's grain. She follows the release teaching. At the meal she intends to ask her daughter Isket never to summon her for a field, even in a bad harvest. She wants Isket to speak of her, use her tools, and remember her mistakes without keeping an emergency task ready for her.

Isket wants to agree and cannot yet do it. During a childhood illness she heard what she believes was her dead grandfather calling her back toward the room. Her mother remembers fever and a neighbor speaking through the wall. Isket's faith rests partly on that disputed kindness. She hopes to offer such care herself when she dies; promising never to ask for it feels like closing a door on someone who might still want to come home.

The grain is ready. Isket has done the work and chosen the food. Davru has invited her brother, who expects to remain an ancestor and is delighted to attend. The meal will happen whether or not Isket can make the promise. Davru must decide whether she can receive it as the guest harvest she wanted.
