# The Turnbooks Of Mere Landing

At Mere Landing, people take little folded books for a walk. Each page shows the view from the next bend of the public gallery: a dark shelf of rock, a pale ridge, then the pale ridge appearing on the other side of the shelf. Visitors stop, turn back, and look again. Residents have favorite places to watch someone encounter this for the first time.

Mere is a small human repair settlement in the [[Worldbuilding/Post-Elysium/index|post-Elysium era]], established after displaced fleets had time to make regular routes. Its enclosed landing and gallery occupy a pocket of stable space beside the unfamiliar geometries called [[Pseudospace]]. Beyond the gallery windows, the usual relationship between a viewer's movement and the apparent positions of distant objects breaks down. The ridge's dark transverse stripe makes it recognizable between views, but those views cannot be joined into an ordinary panorama. The gallery itself is an ordinary continuous passage; a visitor can always turn around and walk back to the landing.

For people whose families came through the [[Welcome to Elysium|displacement into Elysium]], this is a place to spend an afternoon with something their grandparents would have called impossible. Nobody needs to cross the boundary to enjoy it. Children crawl along the low window to find the moment the striped ridge disappears. Some adults quietly do the same.

The first turnbooks were sketches passed among crews waiting for repairs. Mere earns its living fitting replacement assemblies, servicing small vessels, and supplying passing crews; some workshops use [[Wavecrafters|Wavecrafter]] components and their prescribed calibration procedures. Long waits produced careful pictures. Repeat customers began asking what had changed since their last visit, and a few residents learned to make books worth buying.

A turnbook folds like a concertina. Opened gradually, it follows a walk; spread across a table, it puts the incompatible views beside one another. Makers leave broad hinges between pictures rather than invent scenery to join them. The hinge may hold a thumbprint, a note about where to stand, or a drawing of the person who kept getting in the way. Good books give the landscape room, but a completely unoccupied gallery usually means the artist got up unreasonably early.

Mere's enthusiasts enjoy different accomplishments. Some labor over the ridge's stripe and the fine broken edges of the shelf. Others paint the reflections in the glass, including the visitors looking out. Cheap printed outlines let children and impatient adults fill in their own colors. Crews buy unfinished books on arrival and finish them during later calls; recognizing an old patch of paint in the gallery can become part of recognizing the port.

The walk costs nothing. Printing, drawing lessons, refreshments, and occasional original commissions support a few part-time makers alongside their other work. The enclosure needs cleaning and repair whether anyone draws it or not. Visitors who make the walk seated use books drawn from their eye level; a beautiful standing-height sequence can be quite useless to them. Makers learn this from customers more reliably than from praise.

Lately, painter Esra Pell has begun selling books with every view visible on the cover. People buy them because they can immediately see why the walk is worth taking. Her former teacher, Nalin, sells a blank first fold and puts the startling view inside. He likes the hesitation before someone turns a page. Esra likes visitors arriving excited, knowing exactly what they hope to see. Both lead popular walks. Their argument matters to the younger makers choosing whose stall to share, and to a landing deciding whether it wants a famous single image or visitors who return for different walks.

Two developments remain unsettled:

- A regular freighter customer has commissioned a long book of the walk in reverse. Nalin has accepted, then discovered that his familiar sequence gives him very little help deciding where this one should begin.
- Esra has been offered an exhibition in a core settlement. She can take the originals that earn her local lessons their audience, or make a new series for people who may never reach Mere. She has enough spare time for one.
