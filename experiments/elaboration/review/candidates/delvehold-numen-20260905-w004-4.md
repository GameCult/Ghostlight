---
title: The Rain Music of Tovren
description: An orcish pottery town praises a storm goddess by playing with the weather.
---

When rain begins in Tovren, people put bowls outside to hear which ones sing. A thin bowl rings under large drops; a deep storage jar answers with a hollow knocking. Water slowly changes the notes. Under the tiled eaves, someone begins tapping a dry pot, and neighbours arrive with instruments wrapped in their work aprons. On a good evening the rain stops long before anybody wants to go home.

Tovren is an [[Orcs|orcish]] town on clay terraces above an inland river. Its families sell storage pottery and grow reeds on the wet ground below; several workshops still use the kiln sites built by families who settled here together. Children learn the river road by accompanying deliveries, and know which households will let them try an instrument too expensive to touch in the shop. Making a jar that holds grain pays the rent. Making one that sounds beautiful is a reason to stay up after it has been paid.

The town's rain music belongs to the worship of Avar, a [[Gods and Religion|celestial storm goddess]]. Her local image has wind-blown hair and both hands open. Worshippers disagree about whether those hands are giving rain or letting the storm go. Before playing, they address her by name and pour a little clean water over her image. The music that follows is offered to her. Pots played at a wedding or tested in a workshop have no such dedication.

Players leave gaps for the weather. A shower may supply a quick patter against a drummer's slow beat, then suddenly fill every gap at once. People laugh when a carefully rehearsed entrance disappears beneath thunder. Dancers stamp through the dry arcade, turn towards the falling water, and retreat just before soaking their clothes. Some come chiefly to listen. Skilled listeners are cherished: they remember a beautiful passage nobody else noticed and can persuade its embarrassed maker to try it again.

Tovren's congregation gathers beneath substantial roofs, away from the exposed riverbank. Prayer promises no immunity from lightning. During dangerous weather they move into the enclosed shrine and play softly enough to hear anyone calling outside. In a dry month, they pour water through pierced cups into the bowls. They ask Avar for rain, but neither the rhythm nor the arrangement is a [[Mana and Pattern|spell]]. An unanswered evening can still contain excellent music.

Heska, a potter who writes hymns between firings, loves the instant when thunder overwhelms everyone. To her, worship means enjoying a greatness that has purposes beyond keeping Tovren comfortable. She has written a refrain asking Avar to “come with your whole voice.” People who spend their days keeping clay, fuel and finished pots dry sing it with particular relief. For a while, the thing that interrupts their work deserves their complete attention.

The shrine priest, Uren, also dances. He prefers verses in which Avar chooses to soften her voice so small creatures can answer. He hears generosity in a light shower; Heska hears restraint, admirable but only one part of the goddess. Their disagreement has lasted through years of playing together. Uren will sing Heska's melody, but his mouth closes at the invitation.

Now visiting pot sellers are carrying the refrain to other shrines. Heska hopes Tovren will become a place where young musicians spend a season learning, supported by instrument sales. Uren wants to teach there too. He understands that worship helps sustain and shape a god, and worries about teaching distant congregations to recognize Avar chiefly in overwhelming force. He cannot settle that worry by claiming she has forbidden the song. She has given him no such instruction.

Heska thinks his preferred prayers risk teaching people to praise Avar only when she is useful. She has agreed to write another verse, but has yet to find words she believes.

## Openings

- A visiting shrine wants Heska to teach the original refrain during its planting prayers. She must decide what she is asking farmers to invite.
- Uren has composed a quiet response that players genuinely love. Heska wants it sung before her refrain; he intended it to replace that line.
- A dry season leaves the travelling pupils learning with poured water. One has begun writing music for the long silence before rain, and wants it included in worship.
