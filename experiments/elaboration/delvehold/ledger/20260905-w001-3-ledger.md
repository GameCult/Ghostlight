## Idea

Brindlebank is a halfling fruit-tree nursery village whose shared experimental orchard lets residents without land pursue breeding work over decades. Its ambition to plant a second village forces a choice between quickly profitable varieties and giving the next generation room and time to develop its own.

## What life is like

People grow and sell trees, press fruit, travel with orders, mind one another's experiments, and argue over taste and the use of scarce rows. Membership offers continuity for projects too slow for a single wage earner to carry alone. Someone can be valued as a carter, parent, or travelling planter without taking up breeding. The intended tone is warm agricultural ambition with specific disagreements, rather than procedural severity.

## Who benefits / who is harmed / what it costs

Landless residents gain experimental growing space and shared care. Buyers gain propagated stock and experienced planters. Nursery sales sustain the common ground, while fruit products and outside grain purchases support daily life; the village does not subsist on experiments alone. Established orchard owners have income and proven stock that make their preferred expansion easier to finance. Wage workers need subsistence while their trees mature, and the existing tenant shepherd could lose his livelihood if the hillside is planted densely. The immediate scarcity is money to bridge the years before bearing, followed by land, labour, and time. No concealed contract trap is proposed.

## Hooks left

Purchase money competes with settlers' food money; a promising trial ties its collaborators to different desired homes; retaining the shepherd would require a different orchard layout and livelihood mix. Whether the purchase happens, which varieties succeed, and how the community resolves these choices remain open.
