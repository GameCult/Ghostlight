## Idea

In one goblin neighbourhood, short domestic farces turn the habits of loved people into recurring stage characters. A successful impersonation can outlast the relationship or change of heart that made it affectionate, forcing performers to choose what they are willing to notice about their subjects.

## What life is like

Residents admire timing, invention, and precise observation. Affection can mean supplying someone with lines about yourself, lending room for a showing, or carrying a scene to a bedridden neighbour. Performing is optional. Familiar portraits, invented households, private showings, and visiting audiences allow different tastes without requiring a single cultural verdict.

The practice can persist because it uses existing rooms and inexpensive reusable props, draws new material from ongoing relationships, and earns performers attention close to home. Most participants retain ordinary livelihoods; the proposal does not require a professional theatre economy.

## Who benefits / who is harmed / what it costs

Performers gain pleasure and reputation; neighbours gain entertainment and the delight of being recognised. A homebound spectator can remain involved. Subjects can become trapped in a publicly repeated old version of themselves, especially when children take the portrayal as a reliable account. Hosting uses domestic space and lamps; noise competes with rest. Orrik's central cost is more specific: the children conceal pleasures because his sister's successful joke contradicts the father they know.

Vessa can keep a popular part, collaborate on changing it, or develop invented characters. Her pride and attachment to a formerly shared joke make this an actual choice rather than a lesson she has merely failed to learn.
