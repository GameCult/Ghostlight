## What life is like

People enjoy long visits without surrendering all their daily habits. Returning hospitality while still a guest is a pleasure; a grandchild can have a private lunch with a grandparent a few steps from home. Children learn to knock. Some households prefer one busy kitchen and dislike the local assumption that a second is the mark of a generous home. Differences arise from upbringing, money and experience rather than a preference assigned to each species.

Maret hopes to remain someone who feeds her brother sometimes while accepting help when her hands hurt. Hadrin wants to make daily care easier and prevent another dropped kettle. His renovation could remove something she still uses and enjoys. The article leaves a practical trial available without asserting that it will settle every painful morning.

## Who benefits / who is harmed / what it costs

Visitors with unfamiliar hours, returning relatives and adults seeking a nearby independent home benefit. Hosts can sustain longer visits without preparing every meal. Second kitchens cost fuel, space and cleaning; borrowing across yards is less comfortable for people without spare rooms. The fashion can turn money into a judgement about generosity. Maret risks losing a valued capacity; Hadrin contributes care and has committed money to alterations. Neither her preference nor his anxiety establishes the right renovation in advance.
