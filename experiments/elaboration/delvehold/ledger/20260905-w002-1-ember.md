## What life is like

Residents know players personally, learn the game's timing with wooden cups, take sides across household and neighbourhood lines, and enjoy performers who make a difficult catch look careless. Athletic fame is available without becoming a caster. Club collections, shared bells and paid exhibitions sustain the activity beside ordinary work. Nessa wants continued competition; Pella wants a coveted place and her sister's support; Oren wants to win with the routine he has rehearsed. Their desires cannot all be satisfied by declaring the sport safe or unsafe.

## Who benefits / who is harmed / what it costs

Players gain pleasure, recognition and occasional income; audiences and fair exhibitors gain an attraction. Catchers risk collision and hand injury, while training takes time from paid work. The crossing bonus turns one hazardous flourish into a competitive advantage. Removing it may protect match participants while encouraging paid exhibitions elsewhere; retaining it places a novice under pressure to attempt it publicly. Disability does not end Nessa's useful skill, but selection and scoring determine whether her club still values that skill. Oren's frustration is personal ambition, not a hidden institutional plot.
