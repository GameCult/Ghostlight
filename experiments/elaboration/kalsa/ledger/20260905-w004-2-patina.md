## What life is like

Neighbours have familiar work, food to experiment with and jokes whose next lines reward attention. Children can grow into exchanges they once needed explained; knowing the old stories gives the nephew pleasure and confidence. Shared possessions and repeated meals sustain the custom at little material expense. Chavi values spontaneous visits, Udrem enjoys cooking with his daughter, and the nephew wants his own meal to become worth remembering. Their different hopes remain interesting beyond any misunderstanding.

## Who benefits / who is harmed / what it costs

Friends gain a playful way to recall specific shared experiences. Chavi can express affection without making each encounter a direct request. Udrem spends food and attention compensating for absence she has not understood that way; he also risks answering the nephew as though the younger man has merely copied an old joke. Chavi may have to ask for company more explicitly than she likes. The nephew could receive a beloved object yet find its established conversation leaves little room for his food or friends. Nobody has a formal obligation to participate. Clear speech could resolve the portion misunderstanding; it would not decide how these people want their shared humour to change.
