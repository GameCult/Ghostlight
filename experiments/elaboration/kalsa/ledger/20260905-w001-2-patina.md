## What life is like

Warm domestic craft comedy is the chosen tone. People repair baskets, carry goods, make household wares, and occasionally earn money from beautiful objects. They admire patient balance and recognisable gesture, teach children through small achievable pieces, and enjoy showing each other what they made. Families can preserve remembered company, imagine another future, travel as artists, buy unfashionable work, or decline the custom. The craft persists through useful manual skills, cheap reused material, shared meals, pleasure, and modest sales rather than compulsory membership.

## Who benefits / who is harmed / what it costs

Children gain teaching and achievable public recognition; makers gain company, small earnings, and artistic ambitions. Neighbours can collaborate without owning a whole display. Materials, firing, space, and time remain real costs. Those with less leisure may be less visible; helpers can disagree about selling joint work. An estranged person represented in a miniature may experience a public overture as pressure. None of these costs is a concealed purpose of the practice. Retaining a loved piece and funding a learner are both reasonable choices.

## Hooks left

An unwanted reconciliation embodied in a child's figure; a paid travelling display assembled from joint work; and a grandmother's elusive childhood miniature. The last hook can remain about memory and craft: it supplies no evidence of lost magic or an awakening machine.
