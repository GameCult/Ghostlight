# Charter — The Rooms of Vey

A mixed valley culture wants every adult to have a room in which they can receive guests on their own terms. Its public building effort now competes for space and resources with the shared courts that make living close enjoyable.

## What life is like

Adults enjoy giving a meal their own shape while remaining near familiar help. Pots, cushions, shared dough-making, and friends choosing adjoining lives matter independently of civic doctrine. Children learn the distinction between an open door and an invitation. Private-room enthusiasts, large communal households, couples, friends, established occupants, and applicants have different good uses for the same limited space. Warm domestic realism is the intended tonal mode.

## Who benefits / who is harmed / what it costs

Unmarried adults, widowed parents, and resident workers gain hosting authority without needing property or marriage. Current occupants enjoy protected tenure but cannot turn a common-funded room into an inheritance. Crop and cloth contributions, imported roof materials, builders' time, water access, and scarce dry shelf space constrain growth. Dividing courts removes real daily companionship; building farther away consumes more resources and carrying labor. Applicants pay for delay while established occupants can advocate patience cheaply. Meetings offer redress but favor people with time and local relationships. The scope and legitimacy of contribution levels remain a local political issue, not a universal tax settlement.
