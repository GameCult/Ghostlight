# Hearth — The Waking Houses of Clayfold

## Idea

A small Spoker-descended pottery settlement makes kinship through neighbouring households whose waking hours overlap, so children and sick people can find familiar company while their own household sleeps. Courtship and starting a private home become consequential choices because changing when one is awake changes whom one can care for.

## What life is like

People learn quiet competence early: carrying bowls, remembering another person's first-meal preferences and amusing a child without waking exhausted neighbours. They admire useful beauty and reliable company. One can belong through repeated care, join a lover's household circle, keep a private room while sharing meals, or prefer visiting friends. Changing one's sleeping hours is both an intimate gesture and a practical loss to someone else. This supports stories about affection, growing independence and the work of staying close.

## Who benefits / who is harmed / what it costs

Children, sick residents, isolated adults and tired carers gain familiar company. Hospitality takes food, fuel, room and waking attention; poorer households supply more effort where richer ones can purchase help. Reliable neighbours can become assumed carers, and young adults seeking privacy can be made to feel disloyal. The practice persists because its companionship is wanted, alongside its usefulness. Neither refusal nor withdrawal requires a new court or office; the difficult consequence is renegotiating real relationships.

## Rejected first ideas

- Intuition-family games about knowing what loved ones want: would turn unresolved magical experience into a household mind-reading rule and lean on a competence stereotype.
- Spoker households preserving ancient shift duties: would give an invented custom unsupported continuity with the Ark and pull the community back toward technical inheritance.
- A formal entitlement to substitute carers: would reproduce the previous welfare/obligation emphasis. This idea needs neighbours who enjoy and sometimes overexpect one another, not another procedure.
