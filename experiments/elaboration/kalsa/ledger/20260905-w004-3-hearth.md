## What life is like

People develop tastes, routes and reputations, court one another over food, introduce newcomers to favourite cooks, and consider leaving one livelihood for another. Neighbourly belonging can come from making a good inexpensive meal or knowing whom to recommend. Neret wants creative recognition and a viable shop. Osa values the quality of Neret's work as well as personal customers. Their cousin sees skilled cooking as a desirable future; nobody's whole motive is resistance to an institution.

The walk can persist because bakers sell a quick product using remaining oven heat, modest additions make varied meals, and referrals bring several sellers customers. Its social pleasures encourage repeat visits. The seated meal offers a real additional pleasure: simultaneous flavours and uninterrupted conversation. It need not destroy the walk, but its sourcing and recommendations can change who earns from it.

## Who benefits / who is harmed / what it costs

Walkers obtain variety, companionship and local expertise; small sellers gain referrals. The custom requires purchases, preparation and time, so it is not universal free provision. Limited budgets still constrain choice, even if a strip can be shared and inexpensive routes are admired.

A successful shop could employ the cousin, buy predictable quantities from suppliers and attract visitors. Neret bears rent, labour and food costs. Existing sellers could lose personal trade or gain orders, depending on her actual choices. Osa's expected successor has another ambition. These consequences remain contingent; success is not secretly a fraud and tradition is not automatically the kinder option.
